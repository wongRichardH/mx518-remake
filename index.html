Overview

This build recreates the Logitech MX518 shape with a modern internal stack: Kailh GM 4.0 main switches, a PAW3950 sensor and a Nuvoton M483 MCU. The switch and encoder picks still need hands-on comparison before they are final.

Main switches

Preferred order:

  1. Kailh GM 4.0 (Heavy, tactile choice)
  2. Huano Transparent Yellow Shell White Dot (Easy, crispy and less fatigue choice)
  3. Huano Transparent Blue Shell Pink Dot (Medium, softer and more controlled choice)
  4. Omron D2FC-F-7N (G1) (OG switch, not my preferred choice for modern MX518)

Kailh GM 4.0 is the first choice: the closest available match to the Kailh GX, with a firmer, more pronounced tactile click.

Huano Yellow is second, a crisp, light click with fast rebound used in newer mice such as the Keychron G3.

Huano Blue Shell Pink Dot is third, a softer, more controlled click that may be more comfortable over long sessions.

Omron D2FC-F-7N, the original MX518 switch, is fourth.

Side switches

Preferred: Kailh GM 2.0

Kailh GM 2.0 gives a crisp, tactile click and avoids a spongy feel. It is already proven in the OP1 family.

Trade-offs: Keeps the proven reference design. Compare it against Huano switches for sound and tactile feedback.

Middle click

Preferred: Kailh GM 2.0

Same choice and reasoning as the side switches: a crisp, tactile click without the spongy feel common in middle clicks.

Trade-offs: May need a different PCB footprint and button mechanism. Prioritize travel and tactility over brand.

DPI buttons (optional)

Preferred: Low-profile tactile switches

Short actuation and a compact footprint let the dedicated DPI buttons be pressed without disturbing grip.

Trade-offs: Aim for lighter actuation than the main clicks, but not so light that they trigger by accident.

Open question: Does the build need to stay fully faithful to the original design? DPI can be set in the mouse software, and dedicated DPI switches aren't used in practice. Keeping them gives a more accurate MX518 replica but adds hardware design complexity.

Encoder

Preferred: TTC Gold Dustproof

TTC Gold is pretty much the standard premium encoder, meant to give a more deliberate and defined scrolling feel. It comes in several heights, commonly 9 mm, 10 mm and 11 mm.

Sensor

Preferred order:

  1. PAW3950
  2. PAW3395
  • Chip price: On Alibaba the PAW3950 runs about $8.20–$10.50 per chip versus $7.70–$9.40 for the PAW3395, roughly $0.50–$1 more.
  • Mouse premium: Brands charge noticeably more when a mouse moves from the PAW3395 to the PAW3950: the OP1 8K went from $74.99 to $89.99 for the v2 (+$15), and the Lamzu Maya from $93.99 to $119.99 for the Maya X (+$26, though it also bundles an 8K dongle). Most of that premium comes from other upgrades, not the sensor's cost.

The PAW3950 is PixArt's newer flagship and the sensor the OP1 8K v2 itself uses, so it keeps this build closest to the reference. The PAW3395 is the fallback: an older flagship with very established implementations and low power draw.

Trade-offs: The PAW3950 adds headroom (30,000 DPI, 750 IPS) that is rarely noticeable in everyday tracking, and its ecosystem is newer. Datasheet and firmware access must be verified for either sensor.

MCU

Preferred: Nuvoton M483

The Nuvoton M483 is a high-speed USB-capable MCU already proven in a commercial 8K gaming mouse.

Trade-offs: Keeps the proven architecture, but needs firmware development, a USB HID implementation and selection of the exact MCU variant.

Cable

Preferred: Lifted Paracord

Paracord is light and flexible and doesn't get in the way

Wireless build

The wireless build keeps the wired build's switches, encoder and sensor. What changes is the electronics: a wireless MCU in the mouse, a separate 8K receiver dongle, and a battery with its charging and power circuitry. The target is 8,000 Hz wireless polling.

Carries over unchanged

Kailh GM 4.0 main switches, Kailh GM 2.0 side and middle-click switches, low-profile DPI tactile switches, TTC Gold Dustproof encoder and the PAW3950 sensor. The sensor still needs firmware support for the wireless MCU.

Reference architecture

The Endgame Gear OP1w v2 documents a usable split: an nRF52840 in the mouse, and an nRF52820 radio plus a Nuvoton M483 in the dongle for the USB link to the PC. The M483 doesn't disappear; it moves to the receiver.
